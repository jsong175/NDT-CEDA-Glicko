How these ratings work

Why individuals, not teams

Partnerships churn constantly — people switch partners mid-season, graduate, transfer, or debate with three different people in a year. Rating partnerships would throw away everything each debater did with a previous partner. So every round is credited to the four individuals in it, and a “team” rating is assembled on demand from whichever two people you name.

Glicko-2, not plain Elo

Plain Elo carries a single number per player and moves it by a fixed K after every result. That mis-handles exactly the cases this circuit is full of: a first-year with four rounds on record, and a senior returning in February after a fall away. Glicko-2 carries three numbers instead:

  • Rating — the skill estimate, starting at 1500.
  • RD (rating deviation) — how unsure we are. A debater's true skill is inside rating ± 2 RD about 95% of the time. RD shrinks as you debate and widens while you don't.
  • Volatility — how erratic the results have been, which controls how violently the rating is allowed to swing.

A win against a very uncertain opponent moves you less than a win against a well-established one, because it tells us less.

Getting individual ratings out of 2-on-2 rounds

A team's strength is the average of its two debaters. The wrinkle is that you are only half of your team, so your rating moves the outcome only half as much as it would in a one-on-one game. The update accounts for that explicitly: expectations are formed at team level, and each partner absorbs the surprise at slope 1/2.

This matters more than it sounds. Skipping it — judging each debater individually against the opposing team — punishes strong debaters for carrying weaker partners and rewards the partner, and in testing against known simulated skill it squashed the entire rating scale to less than half its true spread.

Rating periods, layoffs, and seasons

One tournament is one rating period: every round you take there is scored against the ratings as they stood before the tournament, so a tournament's own results never contaminate each other. Sit out a while and your RD widens by the month, not by the tournament — there are ~40 tournaments a year and nobody attends more than a third of them. Between seasons every rating is pulled 25% back toward 1500 and RD is reopened to at least 150: the topic changes, the field turns over, and April is weak evidence about September.

Side bias

The aff win rate is measured from the data rather than assumed, and folded into both the predictions and the updates, so winning on the aff is worth slightly less than winning on the neg when the aff is in fact favoured.

Known limits

  • Judges are not modelled. A rating blends debating skill with how well a debater's style matches the judges they happened to draw.
  • Only rated divisions count. Open only; JV and novice rounds are collected but excluded, because those pools barely cross over and their ratings would drift on their own scale.
  • Ratings are relative. 1500 is the pool average, not an absolute standard, and it is not comparable across circuits or across eras.
  • Small samples stay provisional. Anyone under 12 rounds, or still carrying an RD wider than 200, is flagged and kept out of the default ranking. That RD bar is set at roughly the uncertainty a debater still has at the round minimum, so it screens out the barely-rated without becoming a second, stricter sample-size rule; it has to stay above the 150 every rating is reopened to between seasons, or the whole pool would read as provisional each September.
